"I reviewed it three times and still can't remember" — the problem usually isn't the number of reviews, but the review method. This article explains from a cognitive science perspective why flip cards are more effective than rereading notes.

## The forgetting curve and spaced repetition

In 1885, Ebbinghaus plotted the forgetting curve through self-experimentation: newly learned material is forgotten by about 40% within 20 minutes, and 70% after one day. But if you review just before you're about to forget, the rate of forgetting slows significantly.

This is the core of **spaced repetition**: retrieving at the critical moment when memory is about to decay strengthens long-term memory. The key isn't how many times you review, but **when** you review.

## Active recall > passive rereading

Cognitive science has a repeatedly verified conclusion: **active recall is far more efficient than passive rereading**.

- Passive rereading: looking at notes, feeling "I know it all". Actually it's just **familiarity**, not real memory.
- Active recall: see the question, recall the answer in your head first, then check. This process strengthens neural connections.

Flip cards are the simplest form of active recall: the front is the prompt, you're forced to recall first, then flip to see the answer. This is several times more efficient than rereading notes.

## Why cards beat mind maps for review

Mind maps are great for **organizing structure** — helping you connect knowledge. But during review, mind maps have a problem: you see all the answers, with no "recall then check" step.

Cards force a "recall and check" loop:

1. See the front (prompt)
2. Actively recall the answer
3. Flip to check
4. Mark whether you've mastered it

Each time this loop executes, memory is reinforced.
